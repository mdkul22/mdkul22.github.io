# **Chapter 1-4**

> [!NOTE] Title
> **_Chapter 1 and 2_** mostly describes the initial process of how the company was created, how it acquired its possessions (Bombay, Madras and Calcutta) and its initial mode of business. We observe how the English, using the instrument of stock purchases to finance expensive expeditions to India and was mostly a high risk, low reward endeavor. Over time, as the spices and fabric increased in demand in Europe, the reward increased. EIC, having a monopoly over this trade and having influential stockholders (English MPs) made sure British growth of wealth (or at least the growth of wealth for the people in power) was directly linked to the growth of EIC. 
**_Confidence:_** one of the factors that deterred the EIC from expanding was by design - the company was risk averse (stockholders). Initially having few resources and entering India during the reign of Aurangzeb, witnessing the massive Mughal armies pushed it to rely on trade to be its mission. No European had attempted the European way of warfare in India, and there was high risk in going to war when the armies it was against overwhelmed them in terms of size.  
The book is written in a very heroes & villains format. Being a complex story the heroes and villains are not purely British and Indian but characters between both these segments that caused strife and bought some semblance of order. Ultimately the story is about how India fell to the company (EIC) so it’s a slow burn of how anarchy slowly seeped into the country starting from Bengal.

The English started off their ventures in India during Elizabethan times, when the Church split occurred. This prevented any trade with the Europeans and forced the English into piracy and in search of global markets. Being low of capital and no funds from the Royal treasury, merchants were induced to pool in their capital and created one of the first open subscription stock for anyone to buy. Objective was to bypass the arabs for spice trade. In order to do that, they required Naval expeditions around the Cape of Good Hope to the western coast of India (Surat). The EIC could not compete with the Dutch VOC for pepper so it resorted to textile / cotton trade.  The initial inroads into India were modest, with EIC struggling to show political weight as compared to the already present Portuguese to curry favor from the Indian Emperor Jahangir. More inroads during this time (early 17th century) in south India where Madras was founded. 1661, Bombay gifted as dowry to the EIC. late 17th century EIC was influential in England as the MPs owned a piece of the pie of the EIC and it was able to influence multiple decisions in the England in favor of it. It suffered a setback when the Company sent a fleet in Bengal to enforce treaties and were annihilated by the Mughal army. This made the EIC look to a more mercantile and peaceful way for managing their assets in India, aiming for profit and business rather than absolute power. Aurangzeb's death in 1707 kickstarted a downhill trajectory for Indian power. 

In terms of trajectories I think the rule of Aurangzeb kickstarted the decline of Indian power. He reminds me of the Roman emperor _Marcus Aurelius_ due to his discipline and stoic manner of conduct. The constant wars both of them conduct to keep order within their empires ended up causing the eventual demise of their empires. Due to Aurangzeb's alienation of his majority Hindu subjects by issuing diktats that included razing temples, defunding Hindu art and forcing _jizya_ tax onto them created problems that still echo in modern India. He was able to expand the empire to a point never before but at the cost of the empire itself. The treasury emptied to a point that defunded the Mughal army. The Marathas were a side effect to all this, adding to the strife in the country. 

## Lack of military innovation
The downfall accelerated due to the increased infighting among the Indian rulers. As rulers saw kingdoms as their personal properties rather than royal privilege, it meant usurpers could easily stake claim to their right. This infighting within the Mughal aristocracy and it's subsequent decentralization caused more issues as European powers were able to take advantage of their technological advantage by offering mercenary services to the highest bidder; causing more infighting. Mughal technology was way behind in the 17th century, though amassing massive armies but unable to catch up with military innovations like the _jazair_ or the _musket_ that enabled the Persians (Nadir Shah) to raid Delhi with impunity and the French to beat off the Mughal cavalry in direct engagements. Both these events highlight the inward outlook of the rulers, thinking of the closest neighbors as natural enemies rather than the Europeans. It would be hard to understand how a trade company could actually beat them, understandably it would be pretty hard for them to comprehend. The Marathas added to the confusion by employing tactics similar to Mongols increasing the overall strife across India.
